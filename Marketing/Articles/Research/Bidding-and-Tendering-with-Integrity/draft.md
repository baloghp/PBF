## v1 (current), about 700 words

**Working title:** The second promise in every bid
*(alternatives: "The bid is a promise about money you do not have yet", "What integrity in a bid actually costs")*

We talk about integrity in bidding as though it were entirely a question about the work.

Do not overstate the team. Do not bury the assumption that makes the number work. Do not promise a date you have already been told is impossible. All true, all worth saying, and all of it is a promise about the work.

There is a second promise in the same document, and almost nobody audits it. It is the promise that this price can be delivered without financing it off the firms below you.

That one does not come due at signature. It comes due months later, in your payment run.

The numbers here are British, because Britain measures this. Large construction firms in the UK paid one in seven invoices late last year. Median time to pay, 33 days.

But the sharper thing is retentions. A retention is money held back from a subcontractor so there is leverage to get defects fixed. Reasonable enough as an idea. The interesting question is where that money actually sits while it is being held, and the UK government went and looked. It mostly sits in the holder's main bank account. Not ring-fenced. Not in a separate account. The research put it plainly: for contractors there is no protection from an insolvency upstream, because retention money is not typically kept separate.

Read that again as a contractor rather than as a policy document. The money you owe your subcontractor is not being kept safe for them. It is your working capital.

So what happens when the firm above goes under? Of the contractors surveyed who had retentions held from them over a three-year period, 44 percent had lost some of it to an insolvency further up the chain. I want to be honest about the shape of that number, because 44 percent sounds like carnage: it happened on roughly one percent of their contracts, and the median loss was about ten thousand pounds each. Rare event, common experience. The government's own estimate of the aggregate was around 229 million pounds a year in retentions never paid because somebody upstream failed.

And it lands on the firms with the least cash and the least leverage. Construction produced more company insolvencies than any other industry in England and Wales, about 3,950 of them, 17 percent of all cases, in the twelve months to November 2025. A lot of those businesses were solvent on paper and broke in cash.

None of which is an argument for being sentimental about your supply chain. It is an argument about what your bid actually said. A price that only works if payment slips downhill has not removed risk from the project. It has moved the risk to whoever can least survive it, and if one of them fails halfway through, the client pays for the stoppage and pays again for the replacement.

Here is the part I find genuinely interesting, because it is already solved.

A project bank account is a ring-fenced account with trust status. The client deposits the certified amount, and everyone named on it gets paid at the same time: main contractor, tier two, tier three. Three to five days, against sub-contract terms that traditionally run sixty to ninety. The main contractor still decides how much each firm is owed. It simply stops deciding when they get it. And if the main contractor goes under, the money sitting there for the supply chain cannot be pulled back out.

Scotland has published guidance on running them. Wales requires them on the construction it funds, and from this year a Welsh public body that leaves the clauses out of a contract over two million pounds has to explain itself to ministers.

The mechanism is not the interesting part, though. The objections are. Because if your price was honest, you lose nothing by having your subcontractors paid on time. The only bid that needs that float is the one that was never quite real.

This is one of the nine things we ask about in Project Contractor of the Year: whether the commercial model kept both sides solvent, and whether the money reached the people who did the work. In nine years of vendor scorecards I have never once seen it asked.

So if you are bidding this quarter, the question is not only whether your number is competitive. It is whether it is still honest by the time it reaches tier three.
