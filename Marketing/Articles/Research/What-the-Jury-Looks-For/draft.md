# Draft — "What the jury looks for" (Defining part 4)

> Status: **v1 is the post**. ~220 words. One dimension only: the deciding question. Do not add the other eight criteria.

## v1 (current) — ~220 words

I keep seeing project write-ups that read like a traffic-light report.

Green on schedule. Green on budget. Client signed. Invoice paid. And then the room goes quiet, because nobody can say what anyone else should actually learn from it.

Here's the thing. The jury for Project Contractor of the Year *does* look at whether the work landed. Delivery still matters. So does how change was handled, whether the cash flow was fair to both sides, whether the contract and the customer–contractor boundary actually worked.

That's the professional layer. It's necessary. It is not the award.

The question that decides it is simpler, and harder: would we publish this as a case study? Did this contractor do something on the seller side of the work that the rest of us should steal — a judgement call, a fairer way to share risk, a practice someone else could pick up and use?

On-time and on-budget get you read. They don't get you remembered.

I wrote earlier about contractors who went beyond the spec — the candour to challenge a brief, the nerve to carry a risk, the foresight to leave a margin. That's the behaviour. This is how the jury looks for it. Not "were there problems?" Problems happen. "When they did, was there something here worth teaching?"

Nominations open 1 September. If a contractor's name is already in your head, start collecting the story. The dashboard will still be in the packet. The story is what the jury will lean on.
