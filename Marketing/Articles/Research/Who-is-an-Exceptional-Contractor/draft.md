## v3 (three stories - current) - ~550 words

Name the best contractor you've ever worked with. I'd bet you're not picturing a deliverable. You're picturing a moment, usually the one where something was going wrong or unexpectedly right.



**1940, the contractor who challenged the order.** The British came to North American Aviation with a simple order: build Curtiss P-40 fighters under licence. "Dutch" Kindelberger said yes, but, "we can build you a better aircraft in the time it'd take us to retool for the P-40." They gave him 120 days. The prototype rolled out in 102. It became the P-51 Mustang, one of the finest fighters of the war. Competent would have been building the P-40 well. Excellence was the candour, and the nerve, to say that we can do better.

**1943, the contractor who carried the risk alone.** Governments needed penicillin at a scale nobody had ever reached, and plenty of chemists said it couldn't be done. Pfizer's board bet about $3 million of the company's own money on an unproven method, deep-tank fermentation, and, with wartime shortages blocking new equipment, gave up the tanks that made its proven, profitable products to do it. One of its own executives listed the ways it could fail ("the yields are low, the isolation difficult, the extraction murder, the purification invites disaster") and then they did it anyway. They set themselves six months; they were running in four. By D-Day, about 90% of the penicillin that went ashore with Allied troops came from that one converted Brooklyn ice plant. Competent would have kept making it safely in flasks. Excellence was absorbing a risk no one was forcing on them, their own capital, their own bet, because the moment demanded it.

**1970, the contractor who planned for what nobody asked about.** Years before Apollo 13 flew, while the Lunar Module still only existed on paper, Grumman's engineers made a quiet call: size its tanks 10-15% larger than the mission needed, just in case the thing ever had to serve as a lifeboat. Nobody made them. Then an oxygen tank exploded 200,000 miles from Earth, three men crowded into a cabin built for two, and that spare margin, plus a CO2 scrubber jury-rigged from a flight-plan cover, a sock and tape, kept them alive for four days. Competent was hitting the spec. Excellence was the foresight to quietly build past it.

Three companies. Three different kinds of excellence:

- North American had the **candour** to say "there's a better way" instead of just following the order.
- Pfizer had the **courage** to carry a risk that could have sunk it.
- Grumman had the **foresight** to leave a margin for the day everything went wrong.

Beyond competent, every time. And notice who we actually remember. The astronauts. The soldiers. The wonder drug. The legendary plane. The contractors who made all of it possible are mostly a footnote.

That's the pattern in Project Business. The best contractor behaviour is quiet: a margin built in, a risk absorbed, an honest "there's a better way." It rarely makes the case study and almost never makes the headline. These are our everyday heroes, and most of them are never named.

That's why we built the Project Contractor of the Year award. We're looking for those quiet stories, the ones that happen off the spec sheet, so we can name the people behind them, lift them up, and turn them back into what they always should have been: examples the rest of us get to learn from.

## v2 (Grumman / Apollo 13 core) - ~200 words

The spacecraft that brought Apollo 13 home was never designed to bring anyone home. It was built to land two men on the Moon and then be thrown away.

Here's the part that gets forgotten. Years before the flight, while the Lunar Module still only existed on paper, the contractors who built it (Grumman) made a quiet call. They sized its tanks 10-15% larger than the mission actually needed, just in case the thing ever had to serve as a lifeboat. Nobody made them. The failure hadn't happened yet.

Then an oxygen tank exploded 200,000 miles from Earth. Three men climbed into a cabin built for two, and that spare margin, plus a CO2 scrubber jury-rigged from a flight-plan cover, a sock and tape, kept them alive for four days.

Nobody remembers the Lunar Module for meeting its spec. They remember what it did when the mission it was built for was already gone.

That's the part that never makes the case study: the judgement, the margin built in "just in case," the honest conversation about risk before anyone's forced to have it. Whether that's the *whole* definition of exceptional can be debated. But it's the part clients remember years later.

That's the behaviour this award was built to notice.
