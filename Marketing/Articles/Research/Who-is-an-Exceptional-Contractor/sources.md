## 🚀 Grumman & Apollo 13

**The contractor:** Grumman Aircraft Engineering Corp. built the Apollo Lunar Module (LM). It was designed to land **two** astronauts on the Moon and support them for **~45 hours**, then be discarded.

**The judgement call (the heart of the post):** Years before the flight, while the LM "existed only on paper," Grumman's engineers deliberately over-sized the consumables tanks. LM chief design engineer **Tom Kelly**: to give the LM a crew-rescue "lifeboat" capability, "some of the LM consumables, such as oxygen, water, and electrical power, would have to be **increased by 10 to 15 percent** above that needed to perform the basic mission… we decided to make the tanks that much larger." Nobody was forced to plan for a failure mode that hadn't happened yet — they built the margin in anyway.

**When it went wrong:** 13 Apr 1970, ~200,000 miles from Earth, an O2 tank in the Command/Service Module exploded. The command module was pronounced "mortally wounded." The three-man crew (Lovell, Swigert, Haise) sheltered in the LM "Aquarius."

**Off the spec sheet:** The LM built for two men for ~45 hours kept three alive for ~90 hours. CO2 built to dangerous levels because the square command-module lithium-hydroxide canisters didn't fit the LM's round openings — so mission control talked the crew through building a scrubber adapter from a flight-plan cover, plastic bags, a sock and tape. All three came home.

## 💊 Pfizer & penicillin — the seller side *absorbing* risk (NOT "fair risk-sharing")

> **Framing correction (27 Jul):** the risk here was **not shared** — Pfizer bore it almost entirely alone, with its own money, ahead of the pack. "Fair risk-sharing" is a customer↔contractor concept and is the *wrong* label for this story. Correct framing: **voluntary risk-bearing by the seller side.** (Save "fair risk-sharing" for a story where the customer and contractor actually split the risk.)

- 1943: US/UK governments appealed to industry to mass-produce penicillin; "many chemists doubted it could be done."
- **What was at risk (four layers):**
  1. **Own cash:** board (chairman George A. Anderson / pres. John L. Smith) risked **~$3M of Pfizer's own money** — Pfizer's history: management put "their own assets as Pfizer stockholders at stake." Not govt money.
  2. **Opportunity cost:** wartime shortages blocked buying new reactors, so they **repurposed the tanks already making proven, profitable products** (citric acid, riboflavin/gluconic acid) — giving up guaranteed earners.
  3. **Technical:** deep-tank submerged fermentation unproven at scale. Smith: "the mould is as temperamental as an opera singer, the yields are low, the isolation difficult, the extraction murder, the purification invites disaster." One contamination = a whole 7,500-gal batch lost; only ~4 parts drug per 10,000 of broth.
  4. **Stranded-asset risk:** drug possibly too unstable to store/ship; a competitor might find a cheaper route, leaving the investment worthless.
- **Timeline:** self-imposed **6-month deadline; McKeen ran it in 4.** Plant opened **1 Mar 1944**; racing the invasion.
- **Result:** ~5× the initial estimate; **~90% of the penicillin that went ashore on D-Day (6 Jun 1944) came from this one converted Brooklyn ice plant.**
- **On govt involvement:** the War Production Board coordinated the broader program from 1943 and gave *some* firms financial assistance (21 of 175 companies reviewed) — but Pfizer's distinctive act was going big on deep-tank fermentation with its **own** capital, first.

## ✈️ North American Aviation & the P-51 Mustang (verified 27 Jul 2026)

- April 1940: the British Purchasing Commission asked NAA (president James "Dutch" Kindelberger) to build Curtiss P-40s under licence. Kindelberger countered that NAA could design a **better** fighter in the time it would take to retool for the P-40. The British agreed on 10 Apr 1940 **on condition the prototype be ready in 120 days.**
- The NA-73X prototype was **rolled out on 9 Sep 1940 — 102 days after contract signing** (engine not yet fitted); first flight 26 Oct 1940. Became the P-51 Mustang, one of the war's great fighters (~16,000 built).
- **Nuance to respect:** historians debate exactly which milestones the "120 days" spans (drawings→rollout vs. order→first flight). Safe framing: *contract set a 120-day deadline; NAA rolled out the prototype in 102 days.* Don't claim "designed and built in 102 days" flat-out.
- Sources: Wikipedia (P-51) <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American_P-51_Mustang>; Joe Baugher <https://www.joebaugher.com/usaf_fighters/p51_1.html>; Osprey (myth-check) <https://www.ospreypublishing.com/ca/osprey-blog/2015/the-birth-to-the-p-51-mustang/>
