## v5 (current), about 1,050 words

We're twenty minutes into the vendor review and I already know how this ends.

Slide 14. Scorecards. Green, green, amber, green. Someone digitised the whole portfolio last year. Planning tool, risk RAG, SLA hits, the lot. It looks serious.

Then procurement asks it.

Who do we keep. Who do we not continue.

And now we're all staring at the same greens. Nobody wants to go first. Because the dashboard is last quarter. It is not the next job.

I keep sitting in these meetings. More tools every year. And the keep-or-drop call still happens on gut, plus whoever complained last.

So what would I actually want on the table before I put a name in the continue column?

**1. Project success.** Did they deliver, and is anything still there after handover? On-time, on-budget. Fine. Necessary. Closing a project cleanly and getting the thing you paid for are not the same event. So a year on: is the client still getting value out of this, or did "success" stop at the acceptance certificate?

**2. Agility and adaptability.** When the brief moved, did they handle it without a circus? Change is the job. FMI looked at this in 2026: contractors with a disciplined change-management process meet or beat schedule about 80 percent of the time. The rest, about 65. So the question isn't whether changes appeared. They always do. When they did, did the relationship hold, or did we start writing letters to each other?

**3. Commercial model.** Was the price real, and did the money reach the people doing the work? This one usually doesn't make the slide. A lowball bid isn't a discount, it's a wild card invoice you haven't received yet. The UK public sector spent fifteen years moving away from lowest-price tendering for exactly that reason: bid unrealistically low and quality slips, unjustified claims multiply, and subcontractors get paid late. Overpricing is the same problem wearing a nicer suit. We just find out later. Also, if our contractor's subcontractors were quietly financing this project, we didn't get a good deal but a fragile one. So: was the price honest, and did their supply chain actually get paid?

**4. Legal soundness.** Did anyone actually understand the contract? Arcadis has spent two years putting the same causes at the top of construction disputes: holes in the documents, and people who didn't follow, or didn't understand, their own obligations. A contract that doesn't match how we really work is a mediation waiting to happen. So: was it complete, and did both sides work to it?

**5. Interface and governance.** Who does what, who decides, who escalates? The customer-contractor line. A team studied eighty-five large international projects and found formal governance at that boundary was one of the strongest things sitting under cost, time and quality. The contract on its own wasn't enough. Somebody has to work the boundary. So: was ours real, or did the RACI live on a kickoff slide while we spent the meeting arguing who caused the delay?

**6. Risk management.** What about the risks that sit between the two companies? Not their internal log. A subcontractor going under. A payment cascade. A hole in a wall doesn't stay on just one side of the wall. Their failure becomes ours. So: did they see it coming, and did they tell us before it landed?

**7. People development.** Did they invest in the people who actually did the work? The Construction Industry Institute put a number on this years ago: a dollar of craft training on a capital project can come back as $1.30 to $3.00. Eleven percent more productivity, twenty-three percent less rework, fewer people walking off. Whether "percent of profit" is a good metric, I don't know. The question underneath it I'm sure about: do they build people, or burn a crew and bid the next job with a new one?

**8. Team and business acumen.** When it would have been easier to just do the work, what did they do? Two things here, and nobody pays for either. First, candour: the dashboard can be green because nobody raised the thing that would have made it amber. PMI once put a number on that silence. More than half the money at risk on projects sits in conversations that never happened. Second, initiative: did they ever arrive with a better way of doing it, or did they only ever execute the ticket? Order-takers are safe and forgettable. So: did they tell us what we didn't want to hear, and bring us what we didn't think to ask for?

**9. Innovation and industry advancement.** Would we tell this story to someone else? On-time gets you a green box. It doesn't tell me whether anyone should copy what they did. McKinsey found more than half the innovations companies adopt come from outside, and that supplier ideas land about 40 percent faster, because someone has already tried them. So: is there anything here worth carrying onto the next project? A judgement call. A fairer way to share risk. A practice someone else could pick up and use.

Nine questions. Not one of them fits in a traffic light.

And they're not my nine. They're the criteria for Project Contractor of the Year, scored by independent assessors.

I'm not claiming any of this exists to make your vendor review easier. It doesn't. It exists to find the project contractors who did something genuinely exceptional and mostly went unnamed for it. But a nomination that stands up to these nine is roughly the file I wish we had on the table right now. Before we sign the next SOW with the wrong firm. Or let the right one walk.

Project Contractor of the Year Award: nominations open **1 September.**

If a name is already in your head, put it forward. Someone who told you the truth when it cost them. Someone who brought you something you never asked for. That's who this is for.
