## v5 (current), about 1,000 words

**Working title:** What we send after we pick a name
*(alternatives: "The file is not a brochure", "Nobody is counting your words". Keep PCoTY out of the headline.)*

We're twenty minutes past the keep-or-drop call.

Someone has a name. Then the room asks the next question.

So what do we send.

I keep sitting in these meetings. I wrote earlier about the nine questions the dashboard cannot answer. Who we keep. Who we let walk. This is the file that would actually sit on the table. Not the case study PDF from last year's bid. Not the three quotes and a timeline that never went wrong.

Somebody asked me what it takes. I said about two thousand words. That was the wrong answer, and not because the number is wrong.

Two thousand is the ceiling on the main narrative, not the target. Five hundred on what made it exemplary. Three hundred on impact. Three hundred on lessons. Those are limits. Nobody scores a point for filling them.

The more useful question is what the file has to do.

**It has to be checkable.** At least two assessors read every nomination independently, and for each of the nine they have to write down the evidence they used. A claim nobody can check is not evidence. It is marketing. Name the organisations. Describe people by role. Give the dates and the sequence. Give us a client contact who will actually answer. "We significantly improved delivery performance" survives exactly as long as it takes somebody to ask: compared to what?

So: if I rang the client tomorrow, could they confirm the story?

**It has to say what actually happened.** What the project was for. The scope. The milestones in order. The week it went sideways, and what you did. A narrative with no events in it reads like a brochure, and a brochure gives an assessor nothing to write down.

One of the sections also asks what value is still there six to twelve months after handover. That is not the same question as what you delivered. PMI found only 17 percent of organisations report high maturity at realising benefits after the project. So if you cannot answer it yet, that is not a small gap in the paperwork. That is the file telling you something about the work.

So: a year on, is anything still there, or did "success" stop at the acceptance certificate?

**It has to feed all nine.** I went through those earlier. Outcomes, agility, the commercial model, legal soundness, the interface, cross-corporate risk, people, conduct, and whether anyone should copy what you did. Every one of them gets scored separately. If nothing in the file speaks to how the money moved between the two companies, or to who decided what at the boundary, that is not a gap in your project. It is a gap in your file. An assessor who finds nothing has to record that they found nothing. Silence is not neutral.

So: which of the nine would they have to leave blank?

**It has to show you were exceptional, not competent.** Competent is the baseline. The baseline does not win. The test I would apply before submitting: if this file could describe any well-run project in your sector, it is not finished.

So: would we tell this story to someone else, or only to the people who were already in the room?

Then there is the part that is not yours.

We email your client representative a link that only they receive. They rate you, they sign, and they agree we can publish a short version of the story if you win. You will not see that link. You cannot forward it, fill it in, or upload it on their behalf.

Which puts two very small things on your critical path.

The first is the email address, and it has to be right, because getting it wrong fails silently. The link goes nowhere. Nobody finds out. Including you.

The second is the warning. An unannounced email asking somebody to rate a supplier through a link is indistinguishable from phishing, and their IT department will agree with me. So tell your client it is coming, who it is from, and roughly when. Pick someone who still works there and still answers email.

And it has to be in by the closing date, the same as everything else. Nominations close 31 October. The client form closes with them.

Which means that deadline is not really yours. It belongs to somebody at another company who does not yet know they are on your critical path. Anyone who has tried to get a customer case study approved knows the shape of this. Casey Hibbard, who does this for a living, says she has seen sign-off take anywhere from an hour to a year. It depends whose desk it lands on. None of those desks are yours.

You do not have to get the file right first time. The platform checks completeness within about a day and flags whatever is missing. That only helps while there is calendar left, for you and for your client. Early and rough beats late and polished, which is not how any of us were trained to treat a deadline.

If more than one name came out of that meeting, you can put more than one file in. Same account. Distinct projects. Each with its own client.

And if it turns out you cannot answer the impact question about your best project of last year, that is worth finding out now. Not because a jury is asking. Because the next client who shortlists you will.

That is the file. Not a brochure. Not a word count.

I wrote earlier that a nomination which stands up to the nine is roughly what I want on the table before we sign the next SOW. This is how you build it. The words are the easy part. The client is not.

Nominations open 1 September and close 31 October, at ittd.space/pbf-pcoty.

Start with the call to your client. The words can wait. They are the only part entirely under your control.
