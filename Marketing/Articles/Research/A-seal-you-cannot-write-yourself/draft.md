## v2 (current), about 1,000 words

**Working title:** A seal you cannot write yourself
*(alternatives: "The case study somebody else signed", "The trophy is the receipt". Keep PCoTY out of the headline.)*

We lost a bid once to a firm whose brochure was worse than ours.

Ours had better photographs. Cleaner diagrams. A case study I had rewritten three times until legal stopped circling the adjectives. Theirs looked like something a project manager had typed on a Sunday. I was annoyed for about a week. Then I sat in the debrief.

They did not win on the PDF.

They won because the buyer already trusted a story we could not touch. Their client had said it out loud. Somebody outside their firm had checked it. Our file was us, talking about us. Theirs had already been through another room.

I smile now when a contractor tells me their marketing is under control. The website. The one-pager. The three logos on the slide. Fine. Necessary. Also the layer almost nobody in the buying seat still leans on.

TrustRadius put a number on this a few years ago. Only about one in seven buyers still consult the materials the vendor wrote about itself when they make the call. The rest are looking for something that did not come from your communications team. A peer. A customer. A third party who had no reason to be kind.

That is the awkward part of being on the seller side of Project Business. You can be excellent and still sound like every other excellent firm, because excellence written by the firm that did the work is the cheapest claim in the pack. Anyone can type it. Legal will even help you soften it until it survives review.

So what actually travels into the next RFP?

Something that survived a judgement you did not chair.

I help run Project Contractor of the Year. Organisers cannot enter it, so there is nothing in this for me except the process. Which is the only part worth arguing about anyway.

Stripped of the ceremony, here is what it does.

You put a real project in. Not the polished version. The one with the week that went sideways.

Then people who do not work for you read it. At least two of them, independently, and every one of them holds a PMP or a PBP. We do not take their word for that either. They give us the credential number and we check it against the registry before they are allowed near a nomination. The people judging your evidence had to produce evidence of their own first.

They score against nine criteria, and for each score they have to write down what in your file supports it. An adjective with nothing behind it scores like an adjective with nothing behind it.

Your client gets a link you never see. They rate the work themselves. You cannot fill it in, forward it, or upload a substitute.

If the file holds up, you present it and take questions. The award is led by Oliver F. Lehmann, who founded the Project Business Foundation, and the result carries his name and the panel's. That is the part people read as decoration. It is the opposite. Named judges carry the cost of getting it wrong. Anonymous praise costs nobody anything, which is roughly what it is worth.

And you do not have to win for this to pay.

Think about what exists at the end of it even if somebody else takes the title. A project file that two verified assessors could not pick apart. A rating your customer wrote and signed, rather than a quote you drafted and asked them to approve. A story that went through a room you did not chair. If you place, a short case profile goes out under the award, not under your letterhead.

That is the asset. The trophy is only the receipt for it.

I have watched contractors treat awards as wallpaper for the office. Fair. Some of them are. This one is different for a selfish reason marketing people understand immediately: the value is not the evening you stand on a stage. The value is a packet that has already been argued with by people who owed you nothing. The next buyer who says "prove it" gets an answer that did not start in your design tool.

There is a contractor I keep thinking about. Not the difficult one. The quiet one. The firm that changed the scope, raised the risk, told the truth, and left the meeting better than they found it. Nobody writes a press release about that. Smooth delivery gets filed under "that's just how it went." A judgement process is one of the few machines that can make that work visible without turning it into a slogan you wrote yourself.

If that firm is yours, or if you are still the one rewriting the case study at midnight because the adjectives will not hold, that is the point of walking in.
