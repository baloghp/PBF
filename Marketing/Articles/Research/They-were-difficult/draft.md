## v4 (current)

**Working title:** They were difficult
*(alternatives: "They told us on a Tuesday", "We used the same word for both". Keep PCoTY out of the headline.)*

They told us on a Tuesday. The date everyone was still quoting was not going to hold.

Someone used the word after the call. Difficult. I did not interrupt that. That is on me.

I smile now when a contractor makes a meeting worse in order to make the project better. That line is true.

Difficult, on that job, was the date. It was also the contractor who will not agree the scope as written, and came back with a better one. The one who put a risk back on the table that we had already closed. The one who told us the team we asked for is the wrong team. None of that made our meetings shorter. All of it could have saved the job.

A quieter firm came in later. Shorter emails. Meetings that ended on time. People said the atmosphere had improved. What had improved was how little warning we received. The next problem reached us when we were already inside it.

I wondered afterwards whether we were just thin-skinned. PMI has been writing about this for years. More than half the money that sits at risk on projects is tied to communication that failed. Not the reporting. The thing nobody would say while there was still time to move.

It is easy to take the wrong lesson from that. That a good contractor is the one who makes you uncomfortable. Some of them are. It is not a requirement.

I have also sat with a contractor who did the same things and never collected the word. They would not agree the scope as written either. They came back with a better one. They put the risk back. They told us the team was wrong. The meeting still ended on time. Nobody went into the corridor afterwards. We left knowing more than we had walked in with, and we did not feel handled. We felt taught.

That is a communication style most of us do not budget for. They collaborate first. They show you the problem in your own language. They give you a way through before they give you the no. You do not walk out thinking they were difficult. You walk out thinking the work got clearer.

Those ones get ignored. There is no story. No reputation. No one to complain about later. Smooth delivery is easy to file under "that's just how it went." It is not. It is the same candour, taught instead of forced.

Some people are hard to work with because they are late, or sloppy, or building an agenda in the background. You already know those people. They are not this. And silence is not this either. The quieter firm was easy. They just stopped telling us the truth.

I should say why this is here, because the rest is easy to misread.

This is from real work I was on. I have taken out the names, the organisations, the sector, and anything else that would let someone from those rooms recognise themselves. I am not writing it to relitigate a job. I am not writing it as a method for choosing suppliers. I help run Project Contractor of the Year. Nominations open on 1 September. I am telling you this because the award is for both of those contractors: the one we labelled, and the one we forgot to notice.

Project Contractor of the Year is not a prize for being pleasant, and it is not a prize for being difficult. It is for contracted work the rest of us should be able to learn from. Changing the scope. Raising the risk. Telling you the team is wrong. If they also left you easier to work with than when you sat down, that is not a lesser version of the same thing. That is the version we skip.

If you have a name like that, you already know it. They are the ones still in your inbox, and not in any case study.
